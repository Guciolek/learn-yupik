# Gemination in Central Alaskan Yup'ik

Gemination (the doubling of a consonant) is **phonemic** in Central Alaskan Yup'ik — it can change the meaning of a word. This is one of the most important phonological features to recognize when reading or hearing the language.

## What is gemination?

A geminated consonant is written as a **doubled letter** and pronounced noticeably longer than a single consonant.

| Single | Geminated | Approx. meaning difference |
|--------|-----------|---------------------------|
| `ata`  | `atta`   | "room" vs. "that's it" (interjection) |
| `pani` | `panni`  | different verb forms |
| `muna`| `munna`  | different noun forms |

## Where does gemination occur?

Gemination can occur:

1. **Morpheme-internally** — as part of the root word (e.g., `agg`- "to open")
2. **Across morpheme boundaries** — when suffixes attach to roots (most common in the grammar)

## Common contexts

### After dropped final consonant

When a CVC root drops its final consonant before a vowel-initial suffix, the underlying consonant often **reappears as a geminate** in the suffix:

```
Root:  angte-  ("to want")
Stem:  anga     (final -e dropped, -t reappears as -tt-)
+ -u  → angtuq  ("he wants it") — pronounced with long -tt-
```

### In possessive forms

Gemination frequently marks possessor agreement on the possessed noun:

| English       | Yup'ik (3sg possessed) |
|---------------|------------------------|
| "house"       | *nunaktuq* → *nunakaa* (his/her house) |
| "coffee"      | *kafi'a* → *kafi'aa*  (his/her coffee) |

The final consonant of the possessed noun often geminates to mark the possessor.

### In verb endings

Many verb endings distinguish:

- **Single** final consonant: 3rd person singular
- **Geminated** final consonant: 4th person (3pl / obviative)

| Ending | Person | Example |
|--------|--------|---------|
| `-tuq` | 3sg    | *tuq*-singular marker |
| `-teng`| 3pl    | geminated variant |

## How to recognize gemination when reading

1. **Look for double letters in the middle or end of a word** (e.g., `nuna`, `aqallru`, `tuquteng`).
2. **Listen for the long consonant** — it sounds like the same consonant held twice as long.
3. **Check the morpheme boundary** — if the doubled letter spans a suffix boundary, it's almost always a geminate from the root's final consonant.
4. **In Yup'ik orthography**, the doubling is always written explicitly; never assume "ng" is "n" + "g" — in Yup'ik, **"ng" is a single phoneme /ŋ/**.

## Quick drills

Try to identify the geminated consonants in these words:

1. *angyaraq* — the doorway
2. *nunakaa* — his/her house
3. *qayassuq* — he is sleeping (note: *-ssu-* in the middle)
4. *aggautaq* — teacher (one who opens)
5. *mavyuuteng* — they went away

(Answers: 1. *-ngy-*; 2. *-kaa*; 3. *-ss-*; 4. *-gg-*; 5. *-vv-*)
